Why counting words is only the beginning of good writing

Every writer has felt it: the essay that must be exactly 1,500 words, the college application capped at 650, the product description that cannot spill past 160 characters. A word counter answers the simplest version of that anxiety — "how long is this?" — in a fraction of a second. But length is never really the question. The real question is whether the length serves the reader, the platform and the purpose. That is where a thoughtful text analyzer earns its place on the writer's desk.

The anatomy of a count

When WordTally reports your statistics, five numbers work together. The word count is the headline figure — the one assignments, editors and publishers quote. The character count matters wherever hard limits are enforced: social media bios, form fields, SMS segments and code comments. Characters without spaces are the currency of translators and many freelance marketplaces, which price work by the character. Sentences reveal structure: a 1,000-word piece made of 40 sentences reads very differently from one made of 12. And paragraphs show breathing room — long, unbroken paragraphs fatigue readers on screens, where white space is a courtesy.

These five figures update live as you type because writing to a target is a feedback loop, not a final audit. A novelist watching a daily goal climb toward 2,000 words writes differently from one who counts at midnight. A student pasting a finished essay into a counter gets a verdict; a student writing inside a counter gets a compass.

Keyword density: seasoning, not stuffing

Search engines long ago stopped rewarding pages that repeat a phrase twenty times. Modern keyword analysis is about proportion and naturalness. Keyword density — the number of times a term appears divided by the total word count, expressed as a percentage — tells you whether your focus topic is present enough to be understood and absent enough to sound human.

As a rule of thumb, a primary keyword density between 1% and 2% reads naturally for most articles. Below 0.5%, the page may drift off-topic; above 3%, readers (and search algorithms) start to notice repetition. WordTally's density table strips out stop words — the the, and, is scaffolding of English — so the top ten list shows the words that actually carry meaning. The two-word phrase view goes further: it catches the multi-word topics you are really writing about, like "content marketing" or "morning routine", which single-word counts would scatter.

Use the table as an editing mirror. If your article about "home composting" never shows compost in the top ten, the draft may be vaguer than you think. If it shows compost at 6%, you are probably repeating yourself and can replace a few instances with pronouns or synonyms. Density is seasoning: essential, but nobody wants a mouthful of salt.

Readability: the Flesch Reading Ease score

In the 1940s, Rudolf Flesch devised a formula that still powers readability analysis today. The Flesch Reading Ease score combines two measurements — average sentence length and average syllables per word — into a single number from 0 to 100. Higher is easier. A score of 90–100 reads like a comic book; 60–70 is plain English suitable for most adults; 30–50 is academic or legal prose that demands concentration.

The matching Flesch-Kincaid grade level translates the same measurements into US school grades: a grade level of 8 means a typical eighth-grader can read the text comfortably. Most professional writing aimed at a general audience targets a Reading Ease of 60–70 and a grade level of 7–9. That is not dumbing down; it is respecting the reader's attention. Insurance policies and government forms score poorly precisely because they were written for the institution, not the human.

How do you raise a low score? Shorten your longest sentences first — WordTally shows your average sentence length so you know where you stand. Then swap a few Latinate mouthfuls for plain words: utilize becomes use, facilitate becomes help. You do not need to rewrite everything; moving a draft from 45 to 62 often takes ten minutes of targeted trimming, and readers feel the difference immediately.

Speaking time: writing for the ear

A script is not an article. Spoken language moves at roughly 130–150 words per minute for a natural conversational pace, slower than the 200–250 words per minute of silent reading, because speech is paced by breath, articulation and the audience's need to process sound in real time. Formal oratory drops toward 110 words per minute; an excited product demo can push past 170.

This matters enormously for anyone who presents: a five-minute conference slot needs roughly 650–700 words at a normal pace, not the 1,200 a writer might draft. Podcasts, wedding toasts, classroom lectures and voiceover scripts all live or die by this arithmetic. WordTally's pace presets let you audition the same text at slow, normal and fast delivery, and the reverse calculator answers the planner's question directly: how many words do I need for a ten-minute talk? Write to that number, rehearse once, and you will land on time.

The 60-character storefront: titles and meta descriptions

Your meta title and description are the only part of your page most searchers ever see. Google renders titles at roughly 600 pixels wide — about 50–60 characters depending on letter widths — and truncates the rest with an ellipsis. Meta descriptions get about 155–160 characters on desktop, fewer on mobile. A brilliant 90-character title that gets cut mid-word at character 58 is, functionally, a 58-character title with an ugly ending.

Character counts alone are an approximation, because Google measures pixels, not characters: a title full of wide capitals and W's exhausts its 600 pixels far sooner than one in narrow lowercase. That is why WordTally measures your title's actual rendered pixel width in the browser, alongside the character count, and shows a live preview styled like a real search result. Aim for 50–60 characters and under 600 pixels for titles; 120–155 characters for descriptions. Put the primary keyword near the front of the title, because even a perfectly sized title competes with a dozen neighbors, and the first three words do most of the persuading.

A quieter way to work

The best writing tools disappear. WordTally keeps your draft in your browser's own storage — nothing is uploaded, nothing is analyzed on a server, and the page works offline once loaded. Set a word goal and watch the green bar fill; it is a small ritual, but rituals are how books get finished. Copy a formatted report when an editor asks for the stats, or download the plain text for your archive.

Counting words will never make a sentence beautiful. But knowing your length, your density, your readability, your speaking time and your snippet — knowing the shape of what you have made — gives every revision a direction. The muse provides the words; the tally keeps them honest.

Frequently asked questions

How does WordTally count words?

Words are sequences of letters and numbers separated by spaces or punctuation, counted with full Unicode support — so accented characters and non-Latin scripts are handled correctly. Hyphenated compounds and contractions count as single words, matching how most word processors behave.

Is my text uploaded or stored anywhere?

No. Every calculation happens locally in your browser with JavaScript. Your draft is saved only in your own browser's local storage so it survives a refresh, and it never travels to any server. You can use WordTally for confidential drafts with confidence.

What is a good Flesch Reading Ease score?

For general audiences, aim for 60–70 ("plain English") with a Flesch-Kincaid grade level of 7–9. Scores of 80+ suit young readers; 50 and below suit academic or professional readers who expect density. Match the score to your audience rather than chasing the highest number.

What keyword density should I aim for?

Around 1–2% for your primary keyword is a healthy, natural range. Below 0.5% the topic may be unclear; above 3% the repetition becomes noticeable to readers and search engines. Focus on covering the topic thoroughly — density is a diagnostic, not a target to game.

How many words do I need for a 5-minute speech?

About 650–750 words at a normal speaking pace of 130–150 words per minute. Formal or slow delivery needs fewer words (around 550), while a fast, energetic pace can stretch to 850. WordTally's speaking-time panel converts both directions: text to minutes, and minutes to a word target.

Why does Google cut off my page title?

Google truncates titles by pixel width (roughly 600 pixels, about 50–60 characters), not by a fixed character count — wide letters like W and capitals consume more space. Keep titles under 60 characters and 600 pixels, put the keyword first, and use WordTally's live SERP preview to check before you publish.
